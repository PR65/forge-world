# Research Note: Physical-Economy Data Sources for Forge World

## 1. Scope

This research note investigates physical-economy data sources across ten core pillars: **materials and minerals, trade, manufacturing, input-output networks, physical facilities, energy, logistics, prices, technology and intellectual property, and trade policy**. The investigation evaluates institutional repositories, public bulk datasets, REST and Swagger application programming interfaces, data granularity, update frequency, licensing terms, and major analytical limitations. The primary objective is to inventory and assess data infrastructure suitable for powering a macro-level physical-industry planning, simulation, and optimization system, designated as Forge World.

---

## 2. Executive findings

The global landscape of physical-economy data is fragmented across international organizations including the United Nations, OECD, World Bank, and International Energy Agency; national statistical agencies such as the U.S. Geological Survey and U.S. Census Bureau; non-governmental asset trackers like Global Energy Monitor; and academic multi-regional input-output research consortia. 

Macro-level trade and price repositories such as UN Comtrade and the World Bank Pink Sheet offer high update frequency and global geographic coverage but lack sub-national or firm-level physical asset granularity. Conversely, facility-level trackers such as Global Energy Monitor provide exact geographical coordinates and capacity metrics for energy and heavy industry assets but lack real-time operational flow tracking. Multi-Regional Input-Output tables from the OECD, EXIOBASE, and WIOD provide the mathematical framework required for physical-monetary mass-balance modeling across sectors, though compilation lags by two to seven years due to national supply-use table reconciliation processes.

While foundational mineral data from the USGS and trade aggregates from UN Comtrade are largely open under Creative Commons or UN terms, high-granularity industrial datasets and commercial maritime logistics involve restrictive subscription licenses. Official trade and production data also suffer from mirror-trade discrepancies, unrecorded informal economic activity, and confidentiality suppression in sensitive defense or critical mineral sectors, requiring explicit modeling and uncertainty quantification.

## 4. Source and dataset inventory

The assessment of source inventories demonstrates a division between public macro-aggregates and specialized micro-datasets. Materials and minerals data rely primarily on the U.S. Geological Survey National Minerals Information Center, which publishes the *Mineral Commodity Summaries* and *World Mineral Production* covering over ninety nonfuel mineral commodities across more than 190 countries. Access is provided via bulk CSV and Excel tables on the web portal without a dedicated REST API. The data is in the public domain but depends on voluntary foreign reporting and lacks operational sub-national granularity.

International trade statistics are centralized in UN Comtrade Plus, managed by the United Nations Statistics Division. It provides global bilateral merchandise trade statistics categorized by Harmonized System codes across monthly and annual frequencies. Access is supported by a modern REST API with Swagger documentation and bulk download portals, licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0. Analytical limitations include reporting delays of 12 to 18 months, mirror-trade discrepancies, and re-export attribution errors.

Manufacturing and industrial production are tracked by UNIDO through INDSTAT, Indices of Industrial Production, and the Monthly Manufacturing Trade Database. Covering over 170 countries under ISIC classifications, UNIDO provides programmatic access via a SwaggerUI REST API and bulk downloads under UN terms of use. Input-output networks are represented by Multi-Regional Input-Output models including OECD ICIO, EXIOBASE, WIOD, and Eora. These models map inter-industry monetary transactions across 44 to 190+ countries and 35 to 163+ sectors. Although essential for physical-monetary mass-balance modeling, they suffer from publication lags of three to five years and rely heavily on mathematical matrix-balancing algorithms.

Energy and physical facilities data are maintained by the International Energy Agency, the U.S. Energy Information Administration, and Global Energy Monitor. While IEA provides comprehensive national energy balances under commercial licensing restrictions, Global Energy Monitor offers open facility-level asset trackers for coal, steel, gas, and power plants under CC BY-NC 4.0 licenses. Commodity prices and logistics are monitored via the World Bank Pink Sheet, IMF primary commodity price indices, and UNCTAD maritime transport reports, providing monthly spot prices and maritime trade indicators. Finally, technology and trade policy data are accessible through WIPO intellectual property statistics and the WTO Integrated Trade Intelligence Portal, offering robust bulk data dumps and API endpoints for patent filings and non-tariff trade measures.

## 5. Relationships and variables useful for Forge World

For an autonomous physical-industry planning and simulation system, data integration requires bridging monetary input-output accounts with physical mass-balance constraints. Macro-level monetary flows must be translated into physical quantities using rigorous mathematical mappings.

The supply chain adjacency matrix, denoted as $A_{ij}$, is derived from OECD ICIO or EXIOBASE tables, defining the physical-monetary input requirement of industry $j$ from industry $i$ per unit of gross output. To convert monetary values into physical mass in metric tons, physical-to-monetary conversion coefficients $\rho_{i}$ are established using USGS mineral data and World Bank Pink Sheet unit values $P_i$. Facility-level capacity utilization $U_k$ links macro-level demand to micro-level physical asset output from Global Energy Monitor and UNIDO indices through the equation:

$$\text{Output}_k = \text{Capacity}_k \times U_k \times \text{Availability}_k$$

Furthermore, logistics bottleneck modeling integrates UNCTAD maritime indices and port-call dwell times as dynamic capacity constraints on raw material import vectors within regional balance equations.

## 6. Gaps, unknowns, and limitations

Significant epistemic gaps persist across the global physical economy. Informal and shadow economic activity accounts for 30 to 50 percent of industrial raw material extraction and artisanal refining in developing regions, operating entirely outside official UNIDO, USGS, or customs reporting mechanisms. True bilateral material flows are frequently obscured by major re-export hubs such as Hong Kong, Singapore, and Rotterdam, hiding the country of origin for critical minerals including rare earths, cobalt, and lithium. 

Additionally, high-resolution conversion efficiencies, such as exact energy and material inputs per ton of green steel or semiconductor wafer fabrication, are proprietary corporate secrets that must be estimated via thermodynamic boundary models. Private supply-chain inventory buffers held by manufacturers remain an unknown, creating vulnerability to macro-level bullwhip effects during supply shocks.

---

## 7. Follow-up leads and recommendations

To operationalize these data sources within Forge World, development should focus on three primary avenues. First, automated ingestion pipelines should be constructed using the UN Comtrade REST API and UNIDO SwaggerUI to maintain rolling historical trade matrices. Second, geospatial cross-referencing should combine Global Energy Monitor facility coordinates with World Bank transport networks to build spatial network graphs of industrial clusters. Third, research should prioritize multi-regional input-output harmonization methodologies that combine EXIOBASE monetary tables with physical mass flows from industrial ecology literature.
