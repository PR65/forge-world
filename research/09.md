# Research Note: Technology Substitution Case Studies in Physical Supply Chains

## 1. Scope

This research note investigates the stream of **technology substitution case studies** within the **FORGE WORLD Wide Research Package**. The focus centers on how industrial systems, energy infrastructure, and manufacturing supply chains navigate severe material and process bottlenecks through deliberate substitution pathways. Specifically, this analysis examines eight core technology substitution domains:
1. **Rare-Earth-Free Motors**: Replacing neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) and dysprosium/terbium permanent magnet motors with induction motors, synchronous reluctance motors (SynRM), and ferrite magnet designs [1] [2].
2. **Battery Chemistry Changes**: Transitioning from nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) and nickel-cobalt-aluminum (NCA) chemistries to lithium iron phosphate (LFP), sodium-ion (SIB) chemistries, and solid-state architectures [3] [4].
3. **Material Substitution**: Swapping critical raw materials (CRMs) such as copper (in wiring/windings) with aluminum, or critical alloys in optoelectronic and energy harvesting devices [5] [6].
4. **Redesigned Semiconductors**: Adopting Wide Bandgap (WBG) semiconductors (Silicon Carbide [SiC] and Gallium Nitride [GaN]) and legacy-node silicon redesigns to withstand higher voltages and reduce thermal loads [7] [8].
5. **Recycling Innovations**: Deploying direct recycling of lithium-ion active materials alongside advanced pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical extraction to recover critical minerals [9] [10].
6. **Process Substitution**: Substituting carbon-based blast furnace reductants with hydrogen-based direct reduction of iron (H-DRI) for green steel production [11] [12].
7. **Alternate Manufacturing**: Utilizing additive manufacturing (AM) / 3D printing and continuous manufacturing processes to eliminate complex tooling and enable localized fabrication [13] [14].
8. **Local Redesign**: Redesigned modular architectures and decentralized supply chains to minimize geopolitical choke points and transport dependencies [15].

Each case study is analyzed through the structural framework of **Bottleneck → Alternative → Tradeoffs → New Dependencies**, ensuring that secondary vulnerabilities introduced by substitution are fully mapped for physical-industry planning systems.

## 2. Executive findings

The global transition toward electrification, decarbonization, and advanced electronics has created profound supply chain vulnerabilities rooted in geopolitical and geological concentration. Industrial systems face severe supply bottlenecks in critical minerals (lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earth elements [REEs] like neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and platinum group metals) as well as refined metallurgical inputs (polysilicon, battery-grade chemicals, high-purity copper). 

Our wide parallel investigation reveals that **technology substitution is rarely a drop-in replacement; it invariably involves performance, capital expenditure, volumetric, or energetic tradeoffs that substitute one dependency for another.** Key executive findings include:

* **Rare-Earth-Free Motors (SynRM & Induction)**: While permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSM) offer superior power density, their reliance on heavy rare earths creates a severe geopolitical bottleneck. Shifting to induction motors or synchronous reluctance motors (SynRM) eliminates REE requirements entirely but introduces new dependencies on advanced power electronics (inverters requiring advanced silicon/SiC chips) and suffers from slightly lower low-speed torque or lower power factor unless optimized [1] [2].
* **Battery Chemistry Shifts (LFP & Sodium-Ion)**: The pivot from NMC to LFP eliminates cobalt and nickel dependencies, reducing raw material cost volatility. Further substitution toward sodium-ion (SIB) eliminates lithium and graphite entirely, relying on abundant sodium and aluminum. However, these substitutions incur volumetric energy density penalties (approximately 20-40% lower than NMC), making them less suitable for long-range heavy-duty transport, while creating new upstream dependencies on sodium compounds and specialized electrolyte precursors [3] [4].
* **Material and Semiconductor Redesign (SiC, GaN, and Al-for-Cu)**: Copper substitution with aluminum in electrical windings reduces cost and weight but increases electrical resistivity and volume requirements, necessitating larger housings. In semiconductors, adopting SiC and GaN replaces silicon in high-voltage and high-frequency power conversion, slashing switching losses by up to 50-80% [7] [8]. However, WBG semiconductor manufacturing depends heavily on high-purity SiC boule growth and gallium feedstock, shifting the bottleneck from silicon wafer availability to specialized crystal growth and epitaxial capacity.
* **Process and Manufacturing Substitution (H-DRI and Additive Manufacturing)**: Hydrogen-based direct reduction of iron (H-DRI) replaces metallurgical coal (coke) as the reducing agent in steelmaking, cutting carbon dioxide emissions by over 95% [11] [12]. The operational bottleneck shifts from coking coal supply chains to massive green hydrogen generation capacity and high-grade iron ore pellet availability. Similarly, additive manufacturing eliminates hard tooling and enables complex internal cooling geometries, but suffers from high unit costs at scale, slow production throughput, and dependence on specialized metal powders [13] [14].

## 5. Relationships and variables useful for Forge World

For integration into a physical-industry planning and simulation system (such as Forge World), the technology substitution stream provides the following quantifiable state variables and relational vectors:

1. **Substitution Elasticity Matrix ($\sigma_{ij}$)**:
   - Quantifies the rate at which material $i$ (e.g., Neodymium, Copper, Cobalt, Coking Coal) can be substituted by alternative $j$ (e.g., Ferrite, Aluminum, LFP/Sodium, Hydrogen) as a function of price ratio spikes and supply restriction indices.
2. **Energy and Volumetric Penalties ($\Delta V, \Delta E$)**:
   - Volumetric penalty factor multiplying spatial footprint when substituting high-performance materials (e.g., Copper to Aluminum winding volume multiplier: $1.60\times$; NMC to Sodium-ion volumetric energy density penalty: $1.35\times$).
3. **Capital Expenditure vs. Operating Expenditure Shift ($\Delta CapEx / OpEx$)**:
   - Process substitutions like H-DRI shift capital intensity upfront (electrolyzers, hydrogen storage, direct reduction shafts) while trading variable feedstock costs (coking coal volatility vs. green hydrogen cost curves).
4. **New Dependency Adjacency Graph**:
   - Mapping upstream nodes: e.g., Shifting from Permanent Magnet Motors to SynRM shifts the critical dependency bottleneck from *Rare Earth Mining & Refining* to *Advanced Semiconductor Inverters* and *High-Grade Electrical Steel Stamping*.

## 6. Gaps and unknowns

* **Commercial Pilot Scalability**: Many advanced substitution technologies (e.g., solid-state sodium batteries, industrial-scale H-DRI steel plants) have been validated at pilot or early commercial scale, but long-term degradation rates under harsh industrial duty cycles remain empirically unquantified.
* **Secondary Supply Chain Bottlenecks**: While primary material bottlenecks (e.g., lithium or rare earths) are bypassed by substitution, the secondary bottlenecks (e.g., high-purity sodium salts, hard carbon anode precursors, SiC crystal boule growth capacity) are themselves subject to concentrated monopolies.
* **Recycling Infrastructure Readiness**: Direct recycling methods promise massive energy savings, but automated sorting and dismantling infrastructure for heterogenous end-of-life assemblies remains immature at a global scale.

## 7. Follow-up leads

* **Deep Dive into Power Electronics Bottlenecks**: Investigate the foundry and epitaxial capacity for Wide Bandgap (SiC/GaN) semiconductors to assess whether semiconductor supply chains will cap rare-earth-free motor adoption.

* **Green Steel Offtake and Hydrogen Infrastructure Corridors**: Monitor the capital deployment and energy grid connection queues for upcoming European and North American H-DRI commercial steel facilities.
